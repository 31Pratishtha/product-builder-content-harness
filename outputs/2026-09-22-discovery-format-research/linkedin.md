# LinkedIn

## Primary: LI-primary

A discovery worksheet can look complete even when our understanding is not.

That is the problem we want to watch for as we try a five-part decision map. The method is untested; our aim is to make the gaps easier to inspect.

For engineers and designers learning product discovery, here is the structure we are considering:

- Trigger: what made the problem matter then?
- Desired progress: what was the person trying to change?
- Alternatives: what did they consider or try?
- Friction: what made those options difficult or risky?
- Action: what happened next, including delay or doing nothing?

Beside each entry, we want the supporting conversation note. Our interpretation belongs on a separate line.

Take an unanswered alternatives field. We would leave it marked "unknown" and ask a follow-up. Filling it with "they had no other option" would turn a gap into an explanation we have not earned.

The same applies to a delay. Until we ask, we do not know what held the person back.

Our first test will be modest: write a plain summary of one conversation, then fill in the map. Does the map change the next product question? Can we point to the note behind that change?

A second look at the notes might explain any improvement. And the map might just add paperwork.

We want to find out whether the structure earns its place before treating it as part of our discovery process.

## Alternate hook: LI-alt

The rule we want to try in product discovery: an empty field stays unknown until the conversation supports an answer.
