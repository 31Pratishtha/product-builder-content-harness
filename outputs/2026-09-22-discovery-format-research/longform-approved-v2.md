# How we plan to review our user interview notes

*A worksheet we're preparing to try, starting with the questions a conversation leaves unanswered.*

We're working on how to turn user conversations into decisions about what to investigate next. For engineers and designers learning product discovery, that means looking beyond the problem someone describes and asking what they have already tried to do about it.

One question in our planning is how to handle the details we didn't collect. Suppose we sit down to review our notes and can't find anything about alternatives. Did the person try another tool? Work around the problem? Leave it alone? If the conversation didn't cover that, we'd need to ask.

We want a way to keep track of those gaps as we review the notes. We've put together a blank worksheet with five fields and a small comparison we plan to run. We haven't tested it yet.

## Following the person's account

We would start by asking about a recent occasion when the problem came up, then follow what happened. What prompted them to do something? What did they try? Where did they get stuck?

GOV.UK's interview guidance recommends open, neutral questions, concrete examples, and follow-ups when something is unclear. It also advises leaving room for the conversation to develop. We're using that guidance to plan the interview. The worksheet is our own proposal for reviewing the notes afterward. [Using in-depth interviews](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews)

That order matters to us. If an interesting detail doesn't fit the worksheet, we'd want to keep it and follow up rather than steer the person toward an answer that fits a field.

## The questions we'd bring to the notes

The decision map has five fields:

1. **Trigger:** what made the problem matter on that occasion?
2. **Desired progress:** what was the person trying to change?
3. **Alternatives:** what did they consider or try, including doing nothing if they describe that choice?
4. **Friction and risk:** what made an option difficult or uncomfortable?
5. **Action:** what happened next?

For each answer, we'd keep a reference to the conversation note that supports it. We'd also preserve whether the person described something they did, something we observed directly, or something they intend to do.

The fields give us a starting point for reading the notes. We expect to leave some unanswered and keep relevant details that fall outside them.

## Working through an unanswered field

Take the alternatives question from the opening. In this hypothetical walkthrough, there is nothing in the notes about what else the person considered.

We would write "not discussed" and add: "What else did you consider or try?" That gives us a specific question to return to. Writing "no alternatives" would hide the missing information.

We'd be equally careful about explaining a delay. If the notes tell us that someone waited before acting, we would still need to ask what happened during that time. A label such as "lack of trust" would remain a possibility to investigate until we had support for it.

Here's the blank structure we'd use for each field:

| Entry | What we'd put there |
| --- | --- |
| Account | What the person described; otherwise, "unknown" |
| Supporting note | Where that detail appears in the notes |
| Possible explanation | Our interpretation, if we have one, labeled as a possibility |
| Follow-up question | What we still need to understand |

We'd keep a space below the five fields for anything else that seems relevant. The example above only illustrates how we would fill the worksheet; it doesn't describe a completed interview.

## Finding out whether it helps

For the first attempt, we plan to use one conversation. Before filling in the map, we'll write a plain summary and the next product question we'd investigate.

Then we'll complete the worksheet and choose the next question again. If it changes, we'll record which entry changed our thinking and why. We'll also note the effort involved and anything the fields left out.

There is a complication with this comparison: the map gets a second reading of the same notes. Any improvement might come from that extra attention. One attempt can help us decide what to change about the exercise, but won't establish that the worksheet is better than a summary.

We'll keep both versions and record whether we'd keep, change, or drop the map. For now, we want to see whether it helps us identify a useful follow-up we would otherwise have missed. We'll need an actual conversation before we can show what happened.
