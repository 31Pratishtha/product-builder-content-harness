# Product Discovery: Leave the Unknowns on the Map

*A five-field worksheet for separating a participant's account from our interpretation, and a small test we plan to run.*

A completed discovery worksheet is not the same thing as a complete explanation.

That is the concern behind a method we want to try: a five-part map of a user's decision, with the supporting notes kept beside it. We have not tested the method yet. We are sharing the questions, the blank worksheet, and what would make us change it.

This is for engineers and designers moving from building a specified solution to deciding which problem to investigate. The practical question is what to carry forward from a conversation without filling its gaps ourselves.

## Start with an occurrence

The UK Government's user research guidance recommends open, neutral questions and attention to concrete accounts rather than generalities. We take that as a starting point for the conversation, not evidence that our worksheet works. [GOV.UK: Using in-depth interviews](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews)

We plan to ask about one recent occurrence of the problem we are exploring. What happened? What did the person do next? Which parts need a follow-up?

The map is something we would fill from the notes. We do not want to turn the conversation into a hunt for five convenient answers.

## The five fields

**Trigger.** What made the problem relevant at that moment? We want to distinguish a general frustration from the occasion the person is describing.

**Desired progress.** What were they trying to change? We would keep their account separate from our preferred product outcome.

**Alternatives.** What did they consider or try? Doing nothing belongs here if it was part of the account. If alternatives never came up, the answer stays unknown.

**Friction and risk.** What made an option difficult or uncomfortable? This field needs the person's explanation. We cannot infer a particular worry simply because an action took time.

**Action.** What happened next? A reported action, a directly observed action, and an intention are different kinds of evidence. We want the notes to preserve that distinction.

These fields are our proposed way to organize the material. We are not presenting them as an exhaustive theory of user behavior.

## How we would handle a gap

Consider the alternatives field while the worksheet is still blank. If our notes contain no discussion of alternatives, there are at least two very different ways to fill it.

We could write "no alternatives." That would assert something we do not know.

Or we could write "not discussed" and add a follow-up question: what else did the person consider?

This is a worksheet illustration, not an interview that happened. Its purpose is to show the decision we want the artifact to enforce: keep missing information visible.

The same discipline applies to psychological explanations. Labels such as "lack of trust" might be questions worth investigating, but we would not insert them as causes without support. Even an explanation the participant gives us remains their account; we may need additional evidence before making a product decision around it.

## The worksheet

For each of the five fields, use these four lines:

- **Account:** what the participant described.
- **Support:** the note reference behind that entry.
- **Interpretation:** what we think it might mean, explicitly labeled.
- **Open question:** what remains unknown.

Leave unsupported entries blank or mark them unknown. Keep relevant details that fall outside the five fields in a separate notes section.

Before filling the map, write a plain summary of the conversation and preserve it. Alongside that summary, record the next product question you would investigate.

Once the map is filled, write the next question again. If it changed, point to the entry that changed it and explain why the difference matters.

That comparison is the useful part of the proposed exercise. A tidier document alone would not be enough reason for us to keep it.

## What this first test cannot tell us

We plan to begin with one conversation. That is a first check on the worksheet, not validation of the method.

The comparison also has a limitation: we would fill the map after writing the summary. The extra attention could produce a new question even without these particular fields. We would record that alternative explanation alongside any apparent benefit.

We also want to notice what the structure leaves out. If a relevant part of the account does not fit, that is a reason to question the worksheet rather than discard the detail.

No map from one conversation will tell us how common a problem is, whether people will adopt a product, or whether a proposed solution will work.

## What we want to keep from the experiment

Our record will contain the summary, the map, the question before and after mapping, and the reason for keeping, changing, or dropping the worksheet.

If we try another version, we will preserve the first one so we can explain what changed.

For a builder considering the same exercise, the smallest useful starting point is one field and its supporting note. Can someone else distinguish the participant's account from your interpretation?

That is the standard we want to test before giving this method a larger role in our discovery work.
