## Starting question

How can product builders use psychology to understand what users actually do, rather than merely collecting opinions about an idea?

## Angle 1: Map the decision, not just the pain

### Core idea

Product discovery often identifies a user problem but stops before explaining the behavior around it. A more useful discovery artifact may be a decision map: what triggered the user, what outcome they wanted, which alternatives they considered, what felt risky, and why they acted or did nothing.

### Target audience

Engineers and designers learning to conduct product discovery instead of immediately building a solution.

### Central promise

A five-part decision map can turn a vague pain point into a more testable explanation of user behavior.

### Working model

1. Trigger: What happened that made the problem matter now?
2. Desired progress: What was the user trying to make easier, safer, faster, or more certain?
3. Alternatives: What did they try, including doing nothing?
4. Friction and risk: What made each option costly or uncomfortable?
5. Decision: What finally caused action, delay, or abandonment?

### Supporting evidence needed

- One real discovery conversation or observed product decision.
- A before-and-after example showing how the decision map changed a product assumption.
- The decision-map artifact, even if it is only a Markdown template.

### Why it fits now

It connects the team's transition from execution to product ownership with a reusable discovery system.

### Risk

Without a real example, it remains a useful hypothesis but may read as a generic interview framework.

## Angle 2: Interest is not the behavior to study

### Core idea

Positive reactions to a product idea are weak discovery signals. The more revealing question is what happens between interest and action: which uncertainty, effort, social risk, switching cost, or missing trigger prevents the user from taking the next step?

### Target audience

Small teams receiving encouraging feedback but struggling to convert it into adoption.

### Central promise

Studying the gap between stated interest and the next observable action can produce better experiments than collecting more positive opinions.

### Supporting evidence needed

- A real example of expressed interest followed by no action.
- The exact next action requested and where the behavior stopped.
- A follow-up conversation or observation that tests possible causes.

### Why it fits now

It addresses a recurring early-builder mistake: treating enthusiasm as validation.

### Risk

The explanation must not guess why users failed to act. Several causes should remain hypotheses until investigated.

## Angle 3: A workaround is a behavioral product brief

### Core idea

When users combine tools, repeat manual steps, or create personal checks, the workaround reveals more than a requested feature. It shows the sequence they value, the failure they fear, and the trade-offs they already accept.

### Target audience

Product-minded engineers and designers deciding what to build from user interviews or workflow observation.

### Central promise

Reading a workaround as behavior can reveal the smallest valuable intervention without copying the workaround literally.

### Supporting evidence needed

- One observed workaround from the team's product work.
- The steps, motivation, cost, and failure mode behind it.
- A product decision influenced by that observation.

### Why it fits now

It can connect product psychology to the team's interest in workflows, handoffs, and lightweight tools.

### Risk

Not every workaround represents demand. It may be rare, low-cost, or specific to one person's context.

## Recommended angle

Start with **Angle 1: Map the decision, not just the pain**. It gives the pillar a clear point of view, serves the primary audience, and naturally produces a reusable artifact. It also creates a foundation for the other two angles: the gap between interest and action is one decision path, while a workaround is evidence inside that path. The main missing ingredient is one real example. Until that exists, public language should present the decision map as the team's current hypothesis or planned discovery method, not a proven framework.

## Three-platform framing for Angle 1

### LinkedIn

**Job:** Help an emerging builder rethink what a useful discovery interview should produce.

**Frame:** A practitioner reflection that contrasts a familiar output, a list of pain points, with a more actionable output, a map of the user's decision.

**Hook direction:** "A pain point tells us what is uncomfortable. It does not yet tell us why someone will change their behavior."

**Body structure:**

1. Name the limitation of stopping at the problem statement.
2. Introduce the five-part decision map.
3. Show how each part changes what the team might test.
4. Acknowledge that the model still needs to be tested in real discovery work.
5. End with the next experiment: use it in one interview and compare what it reveals against a standard notes summary.

**Best artifact:** A compact image or text block showing the five-part decision map.

### X

**Job:** Make the thesis easy to test, challenge, and remember.

**Frame:** A concise standalone claim plus a short thread that expands the model one decision at a time.

**Standalone direction:** "Finding a pain point is not the end of discovery. Map the decision around it: trigger, desired progress, alternatives, perceived risk, and the moment of action or abandonment. That map gives you something a feature request cannot: a behavior to test."

**Thread sequence:**

1. State the difference between identifying pain and explaining behavior.
2. Explain trigger and desired progress.
3. Explain alternatives, including doing nothing.
4. Explain friction, risk, and trust.
5. Show how the resulting map changes an experiment.
6. Close with the evidence boundary: this is a working model the team intends to test.

**Best artifact:** A single decision-map diagram or a filled example once evidence exists.

### Long-form

**Job:** Turn the model into a reusable discovery guide.

**Frame:** A practical essay for engineers and designers who can build solutions but need a stronger bridge from user conversations to product experiments.

**Working title:** "From Pain Points to Decision Maps: A Better Output for Product Discovery"

**Section structure:**

1. Why pain points alone do not explain behavior.
2. The five parts of a user decision.
3. Questions that uncover each part without leading the user.
4. A real worked example from the team's product discovery.
5. How the map changes the next experiment or product decision.
6. Where the model can mislead us.
7. What the team will test next.

**Best artifact:** A blank template followed by one completed, evidence-backed decision map.

## Next evidence to collect

Before promoting this brainstorm, run one discovery conversation around a recent, specific behavior. Ask the participant to reconstruct the last occurrence rather than evaluate a proposed solution. Capture the trigger, desired progress, alternatives, friction or risk, and final decision. That single record will show whether Angle 1 can become credible content or needs to remain an internal hypothesis.
